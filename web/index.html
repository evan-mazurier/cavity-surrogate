How to read the picture

  • The box. A square cavity full of fluid, seen from the side. Three walls are fixed; the top wall (the lid) slides to the right at speed 1 and drags the fluid with it.
  • Colour = speed. Dark blue is fluid nearly at rest, yellow is moving as fast as the lid. Fastest just under the lid, slowest in the eye of the vortex and in the corners.
  • Moving dots = fluid particles carried by the predicted velocity, like dust in the tank. The flow is steady — the colours and lines never change — but the fluid itself circulates through it. Fast under the lid, crawling in the eye.
  • Arrows = direction. The lid pushes fluid right, it turns down the right wall, back along the bottom, up the left: one big clockwise whirlpool.
  • White lines = streamlines, the paths fluid particles follow. Closed loops = recirculation. The + marks the eye of the main vortex.
  • Orange loops = corner eddies, small whirlpools in the bottom corners spinning the opposite way. They grow with Re; above Re ≈ 2000 a third one appears at the top left.

Re (Reynolds number) is the only knob: momentum versus viscosity. Low Re = honey, one lazy vortex sitting high. High Re = water, a stronger vortex that moves to the centre and bigger corner eddies.

Relative error = size of (surrogate − solver) over the size of the solver's field, both velocity components, all 16,384 cells. The difference panel is the same quantity point by point, scaled to its own maximum.

How the check was built

86 solver runs were made. 64 trained the model. 16 were held back at Reynolds numbers between the training ones and 6 sit outside the range (30, 40, 1750–2500). None of those 22 ever touched the model — they only judge it. The card always shows the one nearest the slider.

Inside the training range the surrogate matches the solver to about one part in a million — the solver's own convergence floor. Outside it the error climbs at once: the model interpolates a validated solver, it does not contain the physics. A surrogate you cannot check this way is a guess.

What the model is

The 64 training flows were decomposed into their principal spatial patterns (proper orthogonal decomposition): 20 patterns capture them to better than one part in 10⁸. Each pattern's weight is a smooth curve in log Re (a cubic spline through the 64 known values). Every picture here is mean + Σ aₖ(Re) · patternₖ, computed in this page from 2.7 MB of numbers. Why so good? A steady flow that depends on a single parameter traces a smooth curve through the space of all possible fields; 20 directions are enough to follow it. A 500,000-parameter neural network trained on the same runs reaches 0.7% — a thousand times worse — and invents features the solver does not have (see the repo).
